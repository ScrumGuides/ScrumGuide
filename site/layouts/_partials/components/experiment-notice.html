This site exists to gain feedback on a hypothesis

Can you break my Scrum Guide hypothesis?

This site exists to test one idea about the Scrum Guide, and I want people to try to break it, not agree with it. Nothing here is published by Scrum.org or by the authors of the Scrum Guide.

What's in it for you

  • For teams: you can change how you work, for example async Dailies, no fixed-length Sprints, or AI agents doing the work, without being told you've stopped doing Scrum. The test becomes whether each loop closes, not whether you held the event.
  • For coaches and Practice Owners: when a team says “this bit of Scrum doesn't work for us”, you can tell whether they want to drop a mechanism, which is fine, or a purpose, which isn't. At the moment the Guide treats both the same.
  • For organisations: “are we doing Scrum?” becomes something you can check. The 2026 Guide publishes conditions, each with a stated falsifier, so you assess outcomes instead of auditing events.
  • For Scrum itself: it stops losing people who agree with what it's for but are fed up with the mechanics.

Try it in a minute

Bring one real situation and see whether the 2026 Guide gets it right. For example:

  • “Is it still Scrum if a team does its Daily Scrum asynchronously?” → Yes. The Plan Loop closes when the Product Developers re-plan toward the Cycle Goal based on what they now know. How they do it is up to them, so async works.
  • “If I get feedback on backlog items as they close in continuous delivery, but never meet my stakeholders together, is it still Scrum?” → No. The Direction Loop closes only when what stakeholders did with the Increment has been assessed against the Product Goal, and the Product Goal has been changed or reaffirmed. Feedback on individual items is evidence for that assessment, not the assessment itself.

The hypothesis

We believe that Scrum can be stated as intent alone, with no mechanics, and still tell Scrum from not-Scrum at least as well as the 2020 Guide does. We'll know we're wrong when a real case gets a verdict from the 2026 Guide that experienced practitioners reject, and the 2020 Guide gets it right.

Whether an intent-only Guide would keep people in Scrum is the bigger claim, and this experiment can't test it. That would take real teams using it over time.

The diagnosis. The Scrum Guide's implementation rules now get in the way of what it's trying to achieve, and I think that's a big part of why people walk away from Scrum. They aren't rejecting what Scrum is for. They're rejecting the mechanics, and the Guide gives them no way to tell the two apart.

Why it's urgent. Teams using agentic tooling already break the timeboxes, the team sizes and the one-person-per-accountability rule, and they still achieve exactly what those rules were for. As things stand, the Guide has to say those teams aren't doing Scrum. That's a failure of the Guide, not of the teams.

How it's tested

The test is real cases. You bring a situation. Experienced practitioners each judge independently whether it's Scrum, and their verdicts are compared with what the 2020 and 2026 Guides say. A counter-example is a case where 2026 gets it wrong and 2020 gets it right. One is enough to show a gap. The hypothesis survives only if every counter-example can be fixed in the 2026 Guide without putting a mechanic back.

Supporting evidence, not proof:

  • Why people leave. I'll ask people who have left Scrum why, and whether they still agree with its intent, alongside existing data on why teams abandon it. This can show the diagnosis is wrong, if most reject the intent. It can't show it's right: the people who answer are self-selected, and stated reasons aren't always the real ones.
  • Agentic teams. I'll collect cases that 2020 calls not-Scrum and practitioners call Scrum in spirit. If hardly any real teams turn up, the urgency is overstated.

Whichever way the results go, I'll publish them on this site.

Liberties taken

It is my experiment, after all. “Sprint” is gone as an element, and with it the repeated fixed length; a Cycle is just the span between two goal-settings. Events are now loops, each defined by what closes it rather than when it happens. And “Scrum Master” is now the Practice Owner, which, unlike the 2020 accountability, has authority to make sure the loops close. That last one is a deliberate change to Scrum, not a clarification.

What I'm asking for

There are two separate questions here, and you may only be able to answer one:

  • Is the diagnosis right? Do people leave Scrum because of the mechanics rather than the intent?
  • Does the 2026 Guide get the abstraction right? Does it state Scrum's purpose from first principles, or just restate the mechanics in vaguer words? Has any purpose been lost, or a mechanism slipped in disguised as an intent? Does it ever let through something clearly not Scrum, or rule out something clearly Scrum?

Feedback on the idea, not copy-editing.

What would prove it wrong

  • Teams that dropped Scrum because they rejected the intent, not the mechanics. That kills the diagnosis outright.
  • A better explanation of the decline: certification, how Scrum gets imposed, the decay of “agile” as a brand — something where the rules aren't the cause.
  • A constraint in the 2020 Guide that isn't mechanical: where the number is the intent, and removing it destroys something.
  • A statement in the 2020 Guide that contradicts an intent in 2026 — then either the base is wrong or the 2020 Guide isn't a conforming implementation, and both are interesting.
  • An argument that reducing to intent makes Scrum unfalsifiable — that the constraints are exactly what make conformance checkable.
  • Evidence that agentic teams aren't straining the constraints, or that this is no different from distributed teams or DevOps, which the Guide absorbed without changing shape.

Try to break it, not agree with it. One good counter-example is worth more to me than a full review.

What is here

  • Scrum Guide 2026 — states what each element must achieve, deliberately silent on how. It's a working draft to show the idea, not a proposed release.
  • Getting Started for Scrum Teams — a straight copy of the 2020 Scrum Guide, unchanged. It is not a new guide, and no work has gone into it as one. It is here to show that everything taken out of the base lands intact somewhere, with nothing left over and nothing lost.

The structure

The Scrum Guide as a base class, with concrete things beneath it:

  • Implementations supply the how, for a context. An implementation can add to what the base requires, or constrain it further. It cannot override or weaken it.
  • Extensions reach beyond what Scrum addresses and make no claim to be Scrum.

Two teams can both be doing Scrum correctly and differ on every page of their implementation guide. Neither can differ on the Scrum Guide.

Where the opportunity is

Today every mechanic is welded to the definition, so changing the fifteen minutes means changing the Scrum Guide. Once the mechanics live in an implementation guide, that guide can be revised in small increments, as often as practice moves, while the Scrum Guide stays still and keeps meaning the same thing. The base gets stability; the practice gets to move. Today they share one release cycle and both suffer for it.
